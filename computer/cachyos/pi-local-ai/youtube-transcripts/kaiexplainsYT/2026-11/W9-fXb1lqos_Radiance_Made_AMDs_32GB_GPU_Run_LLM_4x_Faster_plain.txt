Language model inference, which is just the process of running a model, has two completely different phases, and they are limited by completely different things. The first phase is called prefill, and it's the step where the model reads your prompt. So, all those tokens you just pasted in, your repo, your instructions, and your context have to be processed before the model can write a single word back. The second phase is called decode, which is where the model writes the answer one token at a time and sends each word to your screen. And the key insight is that prefill is compute-bound, meaning it is limited by how many math operations the chip can do per second. Decode is memory-bound, meaning it is limited by how fast the chip can read the model weights out of VRAM. So, these two phases hit completely different bottlenecks, and that is exactly what we see in the numbers. So how does radiance get significantly faster on writing? It cheats, but in the best possible way. The trick is called speculative decoding. A small drafter model, called B Flash 2 in this setup, proposes the next seven tokens in one shot, and then the big 27B model checks all seven in a single pass through its weights. The math that makes this work is that checking seven tokens costs about the same as writing one because the expensive part is loading the weights, and that load happens once regardless. So if even three or four of those seven tokens are correct, we get them for free, and if all seven are right, we just wrote seven tokens for the price of one. So the real question is how often the small model guesses right, and the answer depends entirely on what we ask it to generate. And the reason is that prose is predictable in a human way, but not in the mechanical way a small model can easily predict. JSON and code have rigid syntax, so when the model needs to close a bracket, the small model knows it needs to close a bracket, and the guesses land more often.
